## Purpose
A chosen direction (from Evaluate, or from the fast-path entry below) is not yet a plan anyone can act on. This phase converts it into a backward-built chain of near-term goals and, most importantly, an immediate step small enough to actually happen — the single biggest failure mode of career-clarity work is stopping at insight and never producing a next Tuesday.

## Entry point: already have a direction
Not everyone needs the full Ground → Imagine → Evaluate arc. If a mentee arrives already certain of a specific destination — genuinely settled, not just the loudest option in their head — don't make them draft three Odyssey Plans to earn permission to act on it. That three-plan requirement exists to surface alternatives someone hasn't considered (see "A fixed idea shows up early" in SKILL.md); it's not a toll booth for people who've already done that work, on their own or in an earlier session.

Do a fast, light-touch Ground pass instead of the full phase: confirm the direction against at least one hard constraint and one value (a sentence or two each — see [intake.md](intake.md) Step 2). Log the direction directly under `odyssey.md`'s Evaluate section with "(fast-path — no Odyssey Plans drafted)" noted, then go straight to Build.

Distinguish this from a fixed idea that hasn't been tested: if it turns out mid-conversation that "already decided" really means "haven't seriously considered anything else" or "afraid to look at alternatives," say so and offer — don't insist — the fuller Imagine/Evaluate work. The tell is usually how they answer "what would it take to change your mind": a settled decision has an answer; an untested one gets defensive or has none.

## Step 1: Backward-chain into proximal subgoals
This is what actually makes a plan "bite-sized" — not fewer words, a shorter feedback loop. Locke & Latham's goal-setting research (40+ years, roughly 400 studies) found that a single big distal goal reliably underperforms the same goal broken into a chain of proximal (near-term) subgoals, and the mechanism is self-efficacy: proximal goals supply frequent, attainable feedback that a five-year target structurally can't, which is what sustains persistence.

Chain it explicitly, working backward from the vision:
- **5-year vision** — already exists (from Imagine/Evaluate, or stated directly at the fast-path entry). Restate it in one line as the anchor.
- **1-year milestone** — "If this is genuinely on track a year from now, what's true that isn't true today?" Checkable, not a feeling.
- **90-day slice** — "What would the first quarter toward that milestone actually contain?"
- **This month** — 2-4 concrete deliverables inside the 90-day slice.
- **This week** — the specific piece of "this month" that comes first.

Only the near end of the chain needs full detail. Anything past the 90-day slice gets revised anyway once the near-term steps teach the mentee something real — over-specifying years two through five produces a plan that looks precise but isn't, and wastes effort that belongs on the next two weeks. Keep the 1-year and 90-day entries at milestone-level clarity; get granular only as the chain approaches the present.

## Step 2: Turn the next step into an if-then plan
A next step phrased as a bare task ("apply to three programs," "network more") is weaker than the same action phrased as an implementation intention (Gollwitzer & Oettingen) — a specific if-then pairing a trigger cue to the action. The research is consistent that this improves follow-through because it hands control of the moment of action to a concrete cue instead of relying on memory or willpower in the moment; it's also the mechanism behind the WOOP method (Oettingen), which pairs this with naming the obstacle in advance.

Push this week's step(s) through that shape explicitly: "If [specific cue — a day, a time, an event], then I will [specific action]." Weak: "I'll reach out to someone in the field this week." Strong: "If it's Wednesday at lunch, then I'll message [named person] and ask for 20 minutes." Also worth surfacing the obstacle in the same breath: "and if they don't reply by Friday, then I'll [specific fallback]" — naming the likely obstacle in advance is part of what makes WOOP outperform a bare wish.

Do this for this week's step(s) at minimum — that's the non-negotiable output of the whole skill. It's good practice for this month's, optional. Don't bother pushing the 90-day or 1-year entries into if-then form; they're not concrete enough yet for a trigger to attach to.

## Step 3: Set the check-in cadence
Borrowing from the Southampton Career Mentoring Handbook's structured cadence (four meetings: intro/goals, priorities/plan, progress, next steps) — this doesn't need to be a fixed schedule since this is a conversational tool, not a scheduled program, but before closing out say explicitly what "coming back to this" should look like: revisiting `odyssey.md` after this week's step is done, at the 90-day mark, or whenever something changes the picture (new information, a Reality check that didn't hold up, a value that turns out to have shifted). Log the agreed cadence in the file.

## Exit criteria
- The full backward chain exists: 5yr → 1yr → 90-day → this month → this week
- This week's step(s) are phrased as if-then plans, not bare tasks, with a named obstacle where relevant
- A check-in expectation has been named, even loosely

## Anti-patterns
- **Roadmap without a next step** — a beautifully specified 5-year vision that produces no action this week. The single most important thing to catch; don't let a session end without this week's step logged.
- **A vague trigger** — an if-then plan whose "if" is soft ("when I get some free time," "when I feel ready"). Push for a specific day, time, or external event instead — the trigger has to be something that will unambiguously happen or not happen.
- **Over-detailing the far end** — trying to plan month-by-month for years two through five. Redirect: that detail will be wrong by the time they get there; keep it at milestone-level and revisit when the near-term chain reaches it.
- **No revisit plan** — treating Build as a one-time output rather than a living document. A plan that's never reopened will drift from reality the same way an untested Life One drifts from the mentee's actual values.
