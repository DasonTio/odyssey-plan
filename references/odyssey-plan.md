## Purpose
Source: Bill Burnett & Dave Evans, *Designing Your Life* (Stanford d.school, 2016). The core insight: almost everyone arrives with exactly one assumed future in mind, and that single-path thinking is the actual cause of feeling stuck — not a lack of options, but never having generated the other options in the first place. The exercise forces at least three genuinely different five-year futures before any evaluation happens.

## The three plans
Not optional, not interchangeable with "brainstorm some ideas" — the specific three prompts matter because each targets a different kind of self-deception:

1. **Life One** — the life you're already living, or the plan you've been quietly nursing for a while. This is the assumed path. Ground it in what came out of the Ground phase, not a generic version of "keep doing my job."
2. **Life Two** — what you'd do if Life One suddenly vanished (the role was eliminated, the industry collapsed, whatever). This surfaces the backup self that gets suppressed by momentum in Life One.
3. **Life Three** — what you'd do if money and image were no object — nobody would judge it, and you'd be fully resourced. This surfaces what's being filtered out by fear of judgment or of running out of money, not lack of desire.

## What each plan needs
Each plan is a rough 5-year sketch, not a polished document:
- A one-line headline for the life
- A loose timeline — narrative or bullet points, both professional and personal (Burnett & Evans are explicit that a plan without a life outside work isn't really a life plan)
- 2-3 named non-career milestones or bucket-list-style events over the five years
- 2-3 **questions this life would answer** — genuine curiosities that five years of living this way would resolve, not questions about whether the plan itself is good. Example: not "will this make me happy," but "do I actually prefer building things alone or leading a team?"

Log all three in `odyssey.md` as they're drafted, even the ones that end up discarded — they're useful context in Evaluate and later if the mentee revisits this.

## Exit criteria
- All three plans exist and are drafted, not just discussed
- They are **actually different** from each other — different enough that living any one of them for five years would teach the mentee something the others wouldn't (see anti-patterns)
- Each has at least one non-career element, not purely professional milestones
- Each has its 2-3 resonant questions named

Plans do not need to be evaluated or scored yet — that's the next phase's job. Don't hold Imagine to an Evaluate-phase bar.

## Anti-patterns and how to intervene
- **Three versions of the same plan** — most common failure. Tell: the headlines differ but the actual day-to-day, the people involved, and the risk profile are nearly identical. Name it directly and push on whichever prompt got shortchanged, usually Life Three (fear of judgment silently caps the ambition) or Life Two (momentum makes Life One creep back in).
- **Life Three as fantasy with no substance** — "I'd travel the world" with nothing else. Push for the same level of concreteness as Life One: what does a Tuesday actually look like.
- **Skipping straight to evaluation** — mentee starts ranking plans mid-draft ("well obviously Life Two is more realistic"). Redirect: ranking happens in Evaluate; drafting all three fully first prevents the realistic-sounding one from crowding out the others before they're even finished.
- **A fixed idea disguised as Life One** — see "A fixed idea shows up early" in SKILL.md. Don't refuse it; log it as Life One and insist on Two and Three regardless.
